Activity Timeline
One list, in time order, of everything this app recorded. Findings, events, port scans, packet threats and device sightings are all stored in separate tables with their own timestamps, and this is the only screen that interleaves them.

What it is for: answering "what else was happening at the same time". A single alert on its own rarely says much. An alert with a new device appearing four minutes earlier, or a scan you ran yourself two minutes before, usually explains itself. That ordering is the whole value here, so read the rows above and below the one you came for.

Why the list is spread across the window rather than the newest rows. A capture running on this machine writes tens of packets a second; findings and log events arrive far more slowly. Taking the newest rows of all three together would fill the whole screen with packets from the last few seconds and push every finding off the end, which is what this page used to do. The window is therefore cut into equal time slices and each slice keeps its own share, so a finding and the traffic from its own moment appear together. The line above the list says how much of the window is not shown.

The bar under the filters shows whether the sensors were collecting across the window: all collecting, a sensor was quiet, AgentalSec was not running, not checked. A quiet stretch on a green part of the bar is a calm one; on a shaded part, the sensor named under the bar was not recording. Hover a shaded part for the sensor and the reason.

Enrollment
There is no MDM behind this tool, so you are the authority on what belongs here. Naming a device stops the agent re-guessing it every session. Marking one permanent is a stronger statement: it means the device is supposed to be present at all times, and it is what turns an absence into a question instead of a shrug.

The counts below are deliberately not added together. Only the first is a number of devices you should act on.

Only devices with a stable MAC address get listed here. That's why this card can show fewer devices than the device list above. The MAC address is what ties a row to one physical device over time, and some devices don't give us one we can trust:
  • Randomized MAC: most phones, tablets and newer laptops make up a new private address per network, and some rotate it every so often. So one phone can turn up as two or three rows, and marking it permanent wouldn't stick, since next week it shows up as a "new" device. These are counted in the transient tile below, not listed. If you want your own phone listed, turn off "private Wi-Fi address" (or "random hardware address") for this network on the phone and rescan.
  • No MAC: ARP had no entry for it, or it sits behind another router one hop away, where MAC addresses never reach us. Nothing to identify it by, so it goes in the no address tile.
  • Unreadable MAC: something is on record but it isn't a MAC we can read (cut off or junk). We don't guess, so it's not listed. A fresh scan usually fixes it.
  • Retired: devices marked as gone for good. They stay out of this list and stop raising absence alerts.
All of these still show in the device list above, where the Identity column says which group each row is in.

Port Scan Results
This scanner now does TCP and UDP. Every host gets a TCP connect pass over the port set you chose, and a UDP pass over a fixed list of real UDP services with a proper request for each one: DNS, NTP, SNMP, NetBIOS name service, SSDP, mDNS and LLMNR. Rows are tagged port/tcp or port/udp so a result always says which question was asked.

UDP has three answers and only one of them is "open". A row here means the port replied. An ICMP unreachable means nothing is listening. Silence means neither: the service may be listening and quiet, or filtered, or it wanted a payload we did not send. Silent UDP ports are listed under the scan result as udp_no_answer and are never shown as closed. A port outside the UDP list was not asked about over UDP at all, and a full UDP sweep is deliberately not offered: it is hours per host for answers that would almost all be silence. Open TCP ports found on the hosts you scan. Every scan is recorded, so a port that stays open is observed many times; those repeats are collapsed into one row here, with the sighting count beside the timestamp. Nothing is deleted, tick every scan to see each observation on its own line. Ports touched by the scan you just ran flash amber for ten seconds, tagged NEW if this host has never shown that port before, or CONFIRMED if it was already known. Scope decides what a result means: self-scan ran from this machine on itself, which proves a service is listening but says nothing about who can reach it; remote ran across the network and does measure reachability. Scanning a host outside your own network asks for approval first.

Behavioral Baselines
How this works, in plain words. Every address, process, port and user is watched session after session, and the tool writes down what it usually does: how much, how often, at what hours, and to where. That record is its normal. Confidence grows with the number of separate sessions it has been seen in (2 sessions is low, 4 is medium, 6 is high), never with how many times it was polled in one sitting. When something later behaves unlike its own past, the app says so in Recent Deviations below, in a sentence: about 2 steps from usual is worth a look, 3 or more is far from usual. Unusual is not the same as bad; it is a reason to look. Answer with This is fine, or Ask the agent to look into it. Silence is never approval: one nobody answered stays on the list for a week, it does not become normal. A device keeps what was learned at its old addresses.

Detection rules

This is the list of everything the app knows how to spot. Each row is a rule, not an alert. When what a rule watches for happens, the rule raises an alert on the Alerts tab and its Times fired count goes up.

What they are good for. Each rule is one question the app keeps asking about your home, such as "did a new device join" or "did someone add a login key". Open Why it is here under a row to see what the rule protects you from and what to do when it fires.

This tab and Expectations. This tab says what each rule is and lets you switch it off. Expectations says how each rule has been doing, day by day, from your Real and False alarm answers. Find the noisy rule there, mute it here.

How to read a row. Start with What it watches for, which says in plain words what would set the rule off. Severity is how serious its alerts can be. Times fired is how many alerts it has raised, with the ones you have not dealt with yet in brackets. Muted says whether you have switched the rule off.

When the Alerts tab is quiet, it means one of three things: nothing happened, no rule here covers it, or the rule that covers it is muted. This page is how you tell which.

Mute a rule that keeps firing about something you know is fine, such as a backup server that sends a lot every night. The Mute button first lists everything the rule has caught, each address, program, file or account with what happened and the dates it fired, so you can pick the one you know is fine, or everything. Its open alerts close. The sensors keep watching, but the rule raises no alert, no incident and no dot on Expectations. What it would have caught while muted is counted, who and when, but not kept as an alert. Old incidents stay as they were.

Open Why it is here to see what set a rule off, and for a muted rule, what the mute has held back since and when.

Unmute makes the rule fire again and brings back the alerts the mute closed. If a rule is mostly noise but sometimes right, answer False alarm on its alerts instead: the model learns the harmless pattern and holds back only that, where a mute holds back everything. The AI analyst can see what you muted and cannot mute anything itself.

Rows marked Record are not things the app saw. They are a trail of things the app did, such as blocking a device, and they cannot be muted.

A rule name is never reused, so a retired rule still explains old alerts.

Needs your verdict
The question is whether the alarm was right to worry you. Every incident here really happened: a device did join, a program did start. That is not what you are asked. Integrity Journal
Every high-value write is hashed into a chain. Tampering with a finding, an event or a baseline breaks it, so this cannot prevent anything on a machine somebody else controls, it makes it detectable.

An unanchored chain proves less than it looks like it does. Verifying with no anchor only shows the chain is consistent with itself, and a chain rebuilt from scratch by whoever rewrote the data is also consistent with itself. Take an anchor, then copy the hash somewhere this machine cannot reach: your phone, a password manager, another host. An anchor sitting on the same disk as the database catches accidents, not attackers.

        oldest first, never part of a run.
        


        The behavioural baselines are never pruned. What a prune costs
        you is the raw evidence behind a measurement, not the measurement.
        


        There is no prune button here, deliberately. Deletion is the
        only thing in this application that cannot be undone, so it does not
        get a one-click home on the same page as everything else. To prune
        now, stop the app and run python scripts/prune_db.py,
        which shows you the plan before it touches anything.
      

      

      

      

    


    

      
What is on, what is off, and why

      

        A fresh install shows a dashboard of zeros, and zeros look like a
        clean network. Every collector already writes a reason for a human
        when it cannot run. This is the place they are all written down
        together.
        


        Green is running.
        Amber is switched off or unconfigured,
        which is not a fault, most of these are optional and off by
        default. Red is something that is supposed
        to be working and is not.
        


        Most of this is the verdict of somebody else's last check, not a
        live test. Each collector polls on its own timer, so after you fix
        the far end of one, give it a poll cycle before believing the colour.
        Refresh re-reads the page; it does not re-run anyone's poll.
      

      

        
        
        
        
      

      

    


    

      
Keys

      

        Every environment variable this codebase reads, with what it unlocks
        and what happens without it. The list comes from the code and from
        .env.example, not from anything typed into this page, so
        it cannot go stale when a source is added.
        


        Keys are written to .env and never to
        config.json. That split is what makes config.json
        safe to commit. Nothing here ever shows you a key it already holds:
        set or not set, and the last four characters, is enough to tell you
        which one you pasted.
      

      

      

      

    


    

      
What to call you

      

        The analyst sits in front of your network all day and addresses you as
        nobody. This is the one thing in its whole prompt that you said rather
        than something a sensor measured, so it is labelled that way: it is
        evidence of nothing, it is never cited, and it is not a claim
        about who is at the keyboard. Takes effect on your next message.
      

      

        
        
        
      

    


    

      
Shape

      

        The non-secret half, written to config.json. These are
        read once at boot, so every change here lands at the next start
        and this page says so rather than implying otherwise.
        


        Lists and structures are not here on purpose. The monitored host list,
        the probe exclusions and allowed_hosts live in config.json where the
        comments explaining what they cost are sitting next to them, and a
        text box is the wrong shape for any of them.
